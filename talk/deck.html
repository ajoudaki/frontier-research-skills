Open research
with AI agents

Using today's most capable models on hard, open questions
without losing control

Amir Joudaki

October 12, 2026

Hi everyone, I'm Amir. Today's models are smart enough to do real research, but using them on open questions is surprisingly hard: they produce far more than we can check, and their mistakes are silent. In this short talk I'll share the practical habits I developed to do it without losing control. It isn't a principled framework, just what worked for me, mostly in theoretical research, and everything is in a repo linked at the end.

01 · The problem

Smart models fail beautifully.

1 We're handing more and more research to very capable models.

2 Research fails silently: the prose, the figures, even the math look great, while one hidden assumption sinks it.

3 And there's far more output than we can check. The bottleneck becomes you.

How do you raise the signal-to-noise ratio, and stay in control?

A few gems, a lot of hay.

Let's start with where we are today. We're in the middle of a fundamental transition: from writing every line of code, paper and theory ourselves, to delegating much more research to very capable models. The transition is hard, because in research, agents fail silently, and the smarter they get, the harder those failures are to catch. The text looks flawless, the figures look great, even the math looks great at a glance. But the real failure is hidden on page 12, in some assumption that quietly absorbs all the complexity. On top of that, they produce code, theory and papers far faster than we can read. So we end up with a pile of results that may contain some gems, but finding them takes an enormous amount of time: a gem in a haystack. And noise isn't only mistakes: a result can be correct but irrelevant. You lose so much control that it can defeat the purpose of using them at all.

It starts with some very basic techniques. None of this slide is my invention, but these simple things make a tremendous difference: the perfect 80/20. Two matter most. First, leave the chat interface and use a coding agent that runs directly in your repo. Then the theory, the LaTeX, the code, even the results and figures are all in context, and you stop uploading things back and forth. Second, run everything on one always-on machine, a central workstation where everything lives. Agents can work overnight and wait for runs to finish, and even if you dispatch jobs elsewhere, everything comes back to one place. Then a few more basics many researchers don't use: skills; AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md, which every agent reads, so you don't repeat your prompts; routing, so a large skill is split into files and nothing unnecessary gets loaded; and subagents, which come next.

Each starts clean and sees only its own folder. Their errors are independent, so it only takes one to catch the flaw.

Beyond the basics, here's one conceptual point I think many people still underestimate: multiple agents are fundamentally more powerful than one long thread. Not because they're faster. You pay for the tokens either way, and you're rate-limited. The difference is conceptual. A single thread accumulates everything: every dead end, every earlier claim, every sentiment, enthusiasm or pessimism. Once it makes an early mistake, everything is anchored to it, and telling it to be unbiased can't undo that. It doesn't know how. Separate agents get a clean, independent start, especially if you isolate them and decide mechanically what they see. Run four agents, ideally prompted for diversity, and three may miss a flaw while the fourth catches it. You can also give them roles, different angles of attack at the same time. It's asking ten people instead of asking one person ten times. Most of the skills in this repo build on this, so let me go through a few.

They come at two scales. Sometimes you have a targeted, well-scoped question. For that there are two skills: investigate-conjectures sharpens the question, and solve-math-rigorously solves it. But most open research isn't a single question. It's one big open question you push in many directions. For that, organize-research-project develops the project, and review-ai-paper audits the finished paper.

We start with a targeted question. Even a well-scoped research question is usually still open, not exactly pinned down, and when you give it to AI agents it very often gets downgraded. An agent finds a special case or a counterexample by exploiting an assumption you didn't forbid, or solves a much weaker subproblem and presents it as the main result. Little tweaks that kill the question. With many agents it can get worse: the mistakes propagate and reinforce each other, and the goal drifts from what you intended. So the first skill, investigate-conjectures, formulates the problem before any attempt is made. It uses its own intelligence to find a precise formulation, faithful to your original question, with no lazy way to solve it without solving the real problem. What you get isn't a solution. It's a sharpened conjecture, frozen as a contract. Then the second skill, solve-math-rigorously, takes the strongest reading of that problem, tries to fully resolve it, and audits the proposed solution before presenting it.

A fresh reviewer gets the finished paper, not its history.

Now back to the original goal. Most open research questions aren't a single question. You start from one big question and push in many directions. Some succeed, some don't, and from the successful ones you push further. That takes a lot of exploration, and exploration needs fresh, independent thinking: agents biased by a failed attempt keep chasing the same failed route. So organize-research-project keeps the thrusts, or studies, as independent as possible, mechanically limiting each one to its own isolated folder. But a project can't be purely independent, or you never build on what you've found. So the skill balances independent thrusts with an established core of theory or experiments. Results enter the core through a promotion gate: robustly audited, coherent notation, a good interface, and, most importantly, your approval. So the core holds results that are true and worthwhile. That's the answer to the gem in the haystack: if it's in the core, it's worth your time to read. And when you turn the work into a paper, review-ai-paper audits the whole package from every angle: the logic, but also the citations, the literature and the novelty. It's what a researcher with real integrity would do to their own paper.

One last thought. Even after all these steps, the output can still be very hard to read: dozens of symbols encoding something very simple, in weird, non-canonical notation. So one of the most important skills for me is simply being able to read it, having the AI teach me, or explain its own output. I packed everything about how to explain theoretical content to me into skills, just for my own sake, to digest a theorem or a proof.
