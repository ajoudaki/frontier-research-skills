Open-ended research
with AI agents

Using today's most capable models on hard, open questions
without losing control

Amir Joudaki

October 12, 2026

Hi everyone, I'm Amir. Today's models are smart enough to do real research, but using them on open questions is surprisingly hard: they produce far more than we can check, and their mistakes are silent. In this short talk I'll share the practical habits I developed to do it without losing control. It isn't a principled framework, just what worked for me, mostly in theoretical research, and everything is in a repo linked at the end. Let me start with the problem.

01 · The problem

Smart models fail beautifully.

1 We're handing more and more research to very capable models.

2 Research fails silently: the prose, the figures, even the math look great, while one hidden assumption sinks it.

3 And there's far more output than we can check. The bottleneck becomes you.

How do you raise the signal-to-noise ratio, and stay in control?

A gem in the haystack.

Let's start with where we are today. We're in the middle of a fundamental transition: from writing every line of code, paper and theory ourselves, to delegating much more research to very capable models. The transition is hard, because in research, agents fail silently, and the smarter they get, the harder those failures are to catch. The text looks flawless, the figures look great, even the math looks great at a glance. But the real failure is hidden on page 12, in some assumption that quietly absorbs all the complexity. On top of that, they produce code, theory and papers far faster than we can read. So we end up with a pile of results that may contain some gems, but finding them takes an enormous amount of time: a gem in a haystack. And noise isn't only mistakes: a result can be correct but irrelevant. You lose so much control that it can defeat the purpose of using them at all. So the real question is: how do we raise the signal-to-noise ratio, and stay in control?

The first part of the answer is surprisingly basic. None of this slide is my invention, but these simple things make a tremendous difference: the perfect 80/20. Two matter most. First, leave the chat interface and use a coding agent that runs directly in your repo. Then the theory, the LaTeX, the code, even the results and figures are all in context, and you stop uploading things back and forth. Second, run everything on one always-on machine, a central workstation where everything lives. Agents can work overnight and wait for runs to finish, and even if you dispatch jobs elsewhere, everything comes back to one place. Then a few more basics many researchers don't use: skills; AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md, which every agent reads, so you don't repeat your prompts; routing, so a large skill is split into files and nothing unnecessary gets loaded; and subagents, which bring me to my next point.

Subagents deserve their own slide, because here's one conceptual point I think many people still underestimate: multiple agents are fundamentally more powerful than one long thread. Not because they're faster. You pay for the tokens either way, and you're rate-limited. The difference is conceptual. A single thread accumulates everything: every dead end, every earlier claim, every sentiment, enthusiasm or pessimism. Once it makes an early mistake, everything is anchored to it, and telling it to be unbiased can't undo that. It doesn't know how. Separate agents get a clean, independent start, especially if you isolate them and decide mechanically what they see. Run four agents, ideally prompted for diversity, and three may miss a flaw while the fourth catches it. You can also give them roles, different angles of attack at the same time. It's asking ten people instead of asking one person ten times. Most of the skills in this repo build on this. Let me show you how they fit together.

There are five skills, and they work at two scales. Let me go through them in the order you'd use them. For a single question, it starts with formulate-problem, which turns a vague question into a precise one. Then solve-problem solves it, and every step gets checked. Then teach-material makes sure you actually understand the result. A whole project is many such questions: develop-project runs them as separate studies and grows a core of results you can trust. And when it's time for a paper, review-paper audits it, and its findings can start the next round. Let's start with formulate.

A research question usually starts vague, like this cloud. The danger is that a vague question leaves room for answers to an easier question, and an agent will happily take them. The goal is the steelman, not the straw man. So formulate-problem writes a research contract, and each field rules something out. The question itself is pinned down, so an easier target doesn't count. The definitions are fixed, so they can't drift halfway through. The quantifiers keep their order, so 'for all' can't quietly become 'there exists'. The evidence standard says what counts, so a proxy metric doesn't. And the scope is explicit, so one example can't stand in for a whole class. Notice that the vague boundary couldn't tell us whether any of these were in or out. Once they're ruled out, the sharp boundary emerges: one precise conjecture. Then it's frozen. A material change needs your say, and results for a changed question never count as answers to the original. So now we have the right question, but we haven't solved it yet. That's the second stage.

Now we have a frozen question, but nothing is solved yet. That's the second stage. With your go-ahead, the proof search runs as parallel agents, one per route, and each route uses a genuinely different mechanism: duality, induction, an extremal argument. They all get the same contract, and none is told which strategy to favor, so they stay independent. After every round, a registry keeps score. Two routes that turn out to use the same idea are merged. A route that needs a lemma as hard as the theorem itself is marked blocked, but its valid lemmas are kept. Mature routes can borrow from each other: here duality supplies the missing lemma, and induction becomes a candidate proof. But a candidate isn't a proof yet. Separate checks rebuild every implication from the contract, check each cited theorem and its hypotheses, and hunt for counterexamples at the boundary and in degenerate cases. Only what survives gets written up by solve-problem: the plan first, every nontrivial step derived, no 'clearly' in place of a reason, and an answer to exactly the question in the contract. So now the proof is correct. But do you understand it? That's the third stage.

The third stage is about you. A correct proof you don't understand isn't worth much. Here's the kind of line an agent, or a paper, might hand you: private symbols everywhere. teach-material, together with explain-canonically, teaches it in layers. First, notation. It translates into the symbols the field already uses: the parameters theta, the loss L, its gradient. Only a few global symbols, each defined with its type before it's used. Then the idea, before any detail: every step goes downhill, and if the slope can't change too fast, each step lowers the loss by a definite amount. That's also the plan of the proof. Then a toy example that keeps the real structure: a one-dimensional quadratic, where you can see exactly when the iterates shrink to zero. And only then the details. When the argument invokes a theorem, it's stated right there, in the exact form needed, with its hypothesis checked and its conclusion spelled out for our objects. And if you're confused at any point, it rewrites the whole block rather than patching a sentence. Now we've followed one question from start to finish. But a real project is many questions.

So far, that's one question. A real project is many of them, and that's develop-project. It rests on three ideas. From the research question, studies branch out, and the first idea is isolation: each study has its own question, inputs and folder, and never sees the others' work. That way, an agent stuck on one failed idea can't drag the others into it, and you get the widest exploration. Inside each study, the agents formulate, solve and learn. The second idea is the promotion gate. A result enters the project's core only if it's correct, by an independent review; coherent, in notation, interfaces and tests; and relevant, which means your approval. These pass, and the core grows with each one. These fail and stay outside, with their evidence kept. So whatever is in the core is worth your time: that's the signal-to-noise ratio. The third idea: you build on the core. New studies can rely on promoted results, and they can be promoted in turn, so the core keeps growing. And when the core is ready to become a paper, it goes to the last skill.

The last stage is the audit. Once the core becomes a paper, review-paper reads the full submission, code included. It lifts out the three to five claims that matter most, into a ledger. Then it checks each one against its evidence. It re-derives the key proofs: here, the key lemma's proof fails. It retrieves the cited papers and checks that each citation is used correctly: one holds, and one source can't be verified. It audits the code: these baselines are unfair. It rates how plausible each conclusion is separately from how sound its argument is. Each flaw gets a severity: fatal, major or minor. And a fatal flaw cascades to every claim that depends on it: the main theorem falls with the lemma. The verdict says whether the central message stands, and the confidence reflects only what was actually checked. And its findings can start the next round of the project.
