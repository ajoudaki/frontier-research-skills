with AI agents

Using today's most capable models on hard, open questions
without losing control

Hi everyone, I'm Amir. Today's models are smart enough to do real research, but using them on open questions is surprisingly hard: they produce far more than we can check, and their mistakes are silent. In this short talk I'll share the practical habits I developed to do it without losing control. It isn't a principled framework, just what worked for me, mostly in theoretical research, and everything is in a repo linked at the end. Let me start with the problem.

01 · The problem

Smart models fail beautifully.

1 We're handing more and more research to very capable models.

2 Research fails silently: the prose, the figures, even the math look great, while one hidden assumption sinks it.

3 And there's far more output than we can check. The bottleneck becomes you.

A gem in the haystack.

How do we raise the signal-to-noise ratio?
And how do we stay in control?

Let's start with where we are today. We're in the middle of a fundamental transition: from writing every line of code, paper and theory ourselves, to delegating much more research to very capable models. The transition is hard, because in research, agents fail silently, and the smarter they get, the harder those failures are to catch. The text looks flawless, the figures look great, even the math looks great at a glance. But the real failure is hidden on page 12, in some assumption that quietly absorbs all the complexity. On top of that, they produce code, theory and papers far faster than we can read. So we end up with a pile of results that may contain some gems, but finding them takes an enormous amount of time: a gem in a haystack. And noise isn't only mistakes: a result can be correct but irrelevant. You lose so much control that it can defeat the purpose of using them at all. So we have two questions: how do we raise the signal-to-noise ratio, and how do we stay in control?

Here's the strategy at a glance, and it answers both questions. Start from one open-ended research question. We organize it into research thrusts: separate lines of exploration, each pushed forward. Most go step by step; now and then one splits, and some stop. And with some lag, the parts that are fully audited and relevant form the established core. The thrusts keep pushing out, and the core follows, always in one piece. The explored region's shore is where the uncharted waters begin. Instead of reading everything, you read the core: that's the signal-to-noise ratio, and that's how you stay in control. Finally, a part of the core is carved out and audited again, for novelty and significance: is it scientifically worthy? That's the paper, and that's the gem in the haystack. That's the map, the global strategy. But look at one thrust at the edge, pushing out into uncharted waters. This is the frontier of research, and it's hard, even for a very capable model. How do we maximize the chance that it gets through, and that what it finds is correct?

How do we implement all this in practice?

Let's zoom into that one push. It's a hard research barrier, out in uncharted waters, and even a very capable model can fail here. How do we maximize the chance it gets through, and correctly? The answer is a multi-agent engine. First, why not one long thread? It accumulates everything: every dead end, every earlier claim, every sentiment. Once it makes an early mistake, everything after it is anchored to it, and telling it to be unbiased can't undo that. Independent agents start fresh, isolated, from different angles. The first one misses the flaw. So does the second. And the third. The fourth catches it, and one catch is all you need. That's how the skills run parallel proof routes and independent reviews. Agents can also work against each other. An author makes a claim. A critic objects. The author repairs it. And the critic checks again, round after round, until the objections are resolved. A single thread can't argue with itself like that. And it has to be cost-effective. Think of it as a pyramid. You're at the top: judgment and approval. Just below you, the most capable and most expensive models, for what's mission-critical: reviews, final checks, the hardest steps. Below them, capable models for drafting and derivations; at the base, cheaper models for code and routine runs. The pyramid is built so that a failure at a lower level gets caught by the audits above it. And when a claim can be tested, test it: a short script can refute a false claim in seconds, while re-checking a proof takes far longer. So how do we implement all this in practice?

But each thrust is still open: what exactly is its question, and how do we solve it?

Let's start with the main one, the architecture: organize-research-project. A project is one big open question, and the skill rests on three ideas. From the research question, studies branch out, and the first idea is isolation: each study has its own question, inputs and folder, and by default it doesn't see the others' work. With your approval, one study can build on another. That way, an agent stuck on one failed idea can't drag the others into it, and you get the widest exploration. Inside each study, the agents formulate, solve and learn. The second idea is the promotion gate. A result enters the project's core only if it's correct, by an independent review; coherent, in notation, interfaces and tests; and relevant, which means your approval. These pass, and the core grows with each one. These fail and stay outside, with their evidence kept. So whatever is in the core is worth your time: that's the signal-to-noise ratio. The third idea: you build on the core. New studies can rely on promoted results, and they can be promoted in turn, so the core keeps growing. That's the organization, and it already has a lot of value. But each thrust is itself a hard research question. What exactly is its question? And how do we solve it? Let's take one thrust.

Take one thrust. Even a narrowed question is still open, and usually vague, like this cloud. The danger is that a vague question leaves room for answers to an easier question, and an agent will happily take them. The goal is the steelman, not the straw man: the strongest version that stays faithful to what you meant. So formulate-problem writes a research contract, and each field rules something out. The question itself is pinned down, so an easier target doesn't count. The definitions are fixed, so they can't drift halfway through. The quantifiers keep their order, so 'for all' can't quietly become 'there exists'. The evidence standard says what counts, so a proxy metric doesn't. And the scope is explicit, so one example can't stand in for a whole class. Notice that the vague boundary couldn't tell us whether any of these were in or out. Once they're ruled out, the sharp boundary emerges: one precise conjecture. Then it's frozen. A material change needs your say, and results for a changed question never count as answers to the original. Now the question is pinned down, with no wiggle room. But we still have to solve it, and that's the next hard push.

Solved, most likely. But to judge it, we still have to understand it.

Solving is the second hard push, and it starts from the frozen contract. With your go-ahead, the proof search runs as parallel agents, one per route, and each route uses a genuinely different mechanism: duality, induction, an extremal argument. They all get the same contract, and none is told which strategy to favor, so they stay independent. After every round, a registry keeps score. Two routes that turn out to use the same idea are merged. A route that needs a lemma as hard as the theorem itself is marked blocked, but its valid lemmas are kept. Mature routes can borrow from each other: here duality supplies the missing lemma, and induction becomes a candidate proof. But a candidate isn't a proof yet. Separate checks rebuild every implication from the contract, check each cited theorem and its hypotheses, and hunt for counterexamples at the boundary and in degenerate cases. Only what survives gets written up by solve-problem: the plan first, every nontrivial step derived, no 'clearly' in place of a reason, and an answer to exactly the question in the contract. So now the thrust has a written proof that's most likely correct. We've delegated as much as we could, but we still have to stay in charge: someone has to judge whether it's relevant, whether it's the right direction, and whether an assumption or technique was quietly tweaked along the way. That means reading it, and agent output can be very hard to read. That's the third stage.

A correct proof you don't understand isn't worth much. Here's the kind of line an agent, or a paper, might hand you: private symbols everywhere. teach-material, together with explain-canonically, teaches it in layers. First, notation. It translates into the symbols the field already uses: the parameters theta, the loss L, its gradient. Only a few global symbols, each defined with its type before it's used. Then the idea, before any detail: every step goes downhill, and if the slope can't change too fast, each step lowers the loss by a definite amount. That's also the plan of the proof. Then a toy example that keeps the real structure: a one-dimensional quadratic, where you can see exactly when the iterates shrink to zero. And only then the details. When the argument invokes a theorem, it's stated right there, in the exact form needed, with its hypothesis checked and its conclusion spelled out for our objects. And if you're confused at any point, it rewrites the whole block rather than patching a sentence. Once you understand it, you can judge whether it joins the core. And that completes the loop for one thrust.

Now let's put it all together.

So now we know how to run organize-research-project: we make the thrusts, formulate them, solve them, understand them, and promote what's true and relevant into the established core. But is that a scientific result? Not yet. Some results matter more than others, and a paper is judged on more than truth: novelty, significance, the literature. So we carve out the high-value part, and that needs a different kind of audit. review-paper reads the full submission, code included. It lifts out the three to five claims that matter most, into a ledger. Then it checks each one against its evidence. It re-derives the key proofs: here, the key lemma's proof fails. It retrieves the cited papers and checks that each citation is used correctly: one holds, and one source can't be verified. It audits the code: these baselines are unfair. It rates how plausible each conclusion is separately from how sound its argument is. Each flaw gets a severity: fatal, major or minor. And a fatal flaw cascades to every claim that depends on it: the main theorem falls with the lemma. The verdict says whether the central message stands, and the confidence reflects only what was actually checked. And its findings can start the next round of the project. We start from one open-ended question. organize-research-project turns it into research thrusts, and inside each one, formulate, solve and learn. The explored region grows fast, and its shore is where the uncharted waters begin. Behind it, with some lag, the core grows through the promotion gate, always in one piece. That's the balance between exploration and exploitation: fresh, independent exploration out at the edge, and a core you can build on. And every step raises the signal-to-noise ratio: agents in adversarial positions audit each other, keep the spirit of the question, solve it without much help from us, and help us understand the result, so we can judge it quickly. Then review-paper audits the carved-out part as a whole paper: the claims, the literature, the novelty. Only a small part of everything we explored becomes the paper. Reading all of it would have been an enormous effort; reading and digesting this part is not. That's the gem in the haystack. So let me wrap up.
