# Research with AI Agents: speaker notes

## 1. Open-ended research with AI agents
Hi everyone, I'm Amir. Today's models are smart enough to do real research, but using them on open questions is surprisingly hard: they produce far more than we can check, and their mistakes are silent. In this short talk I'll share the practical habits I developed to do it without losing control. It isn't a principled framework, just what worked for me, mostly in theoretical research, and everything is in a repo linked at the end. Let me start with the problem.

## 2. Smart models fail beautifully.
Let's start with where we are today. We're in the middle of a fundamental transition: from writing every line of code, paper and theory ourselves, to delegating much more research to very capable models. The transition is hard, because in research, agents fail silently, and the smarter they get, the harder those failures are to catch. The text looks flawless, the figures look great, even the math looks great at a glance. But the real failure is hidden on page 12, in some assumption that quietly absorbs all the complexity. On top of that, they produce code, theory and papers far faster than we can read. So we end up with a pile of results that may contain some gems, but finding them takes an enormous amount of time: a gem in a haystack. And noise isn't only mistakes: a result can be correct but irrelevant. You lose so much control that it can defeat the purpose of using them at all. So the real question is: how do we raise the signal-to-noise ratio, and stay in control?

## 3. Most of the value is in the basics.
The first part of the answer is surprisingly basic. None of this slide is my invention, but these simple things make a tremendous difference: the perfect 80/20. Two matter most. First, leave the chat interface and use a coding agent that runs directly in your repo. Then the theory, the LaTeX, the code, even the results and figures are all in context, and you stop uploading things back and forth. Second, run everything on one always-on machine, a central workstation where everything lives. Agents can work overnight and wait for runs to finish, and even if you dispatch jobs elsewhere, everything comes back to one place. Then a few more basics many researchers don't use: skills; AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md, which every agent reads, so you don't repeat your prompts; routing, so a large skill is split into files and nothing unnecessary gets loaded; and subagents, which bring me to my next point.

## 4. Many agents beat one long thread.
Subagents deserve their own slide, because here's one conceptual point I think many people still underestimate: multiple agents are fundamentally more powerful than one long thread. Not because they're faster. You pay for the tokens either way, and you're rate-limited. The difference is conceptual. A single thread accumulates everything: every dead end, every earlier claim, every sentiment, enthusiasm or pessimism. Once it makes an early mistake, everything is anchored to it, and telling it to be unbiased can't undo that. It doesn't know how. Separate agents get a clean, independent start, especially if you isolate them and decide mechanically what they see. Run four agents, ideally prompted for diversity, and three may miss a flaw while the fourth catches it. You can also give them roles, different angles of attack at the same time. It's asking ten people instead of asking one person ten times. Most of the skills in this repo build on this. Let me show you how they fit together.

## 5. One question, or a whole project.
They work at two scales. Sometimes you have a targeted, well-scoped question. For that there are three skills: formulate-problem sharpens the question, solve-problem solves it, and teach-material helps you understand the result. But most open-ended research isn't a single question. It's one big open question you push in many directions. For that, develop-project develops the project, and review-paper audits the finished paper. Let's start with the single question.

## 6. Formulate, solve, then learn.
We start with a single, targeted question. Even a well-scoped research question is usually still open, not exactly pinned down, and when you give it to AI agents it very often gets downgraded. An agent finds a special case or a counterexample by exploiting an assumption you didn't forbid, or solves a much weaker subproblem and presents it as the main result. Little tweaks that kill the question. With many agents it can get worse: the mistakes propagate and reinforce each other, and the goal drifts from what you intended. So the first skill, formulate-problem, formulates the problem before any attempt is made. It finds a precise formulation, faithful to your original question, with no lazy way to solve it without solving the real problem, and freezes it as a contract. Then the second skill, solve-problem, takes the strongest reading of that problem, tries to fully resolve it, and audits the proposed solution before presenting it. After these two stages, it's much more likely that we solved the right problem, and that the proof is correct. But we still have to understand it, as efficiently as possible, and the output can be very hard to read: dozens of symbols encoding something simple, in weird notation. That's the third skill, teach-material. It teaches me the result one block at a time, defines every symbol before it's used, and when I'm confused, it rebuilds the explanation instead of patching it. That's one question. Now let's zoom out to a whole project.

## 7. Develop in isolation, then audit cold.
Most open-ended research questions aren't a single question. You start from one big question and push in many directions. Some succeed, some don't, and from the successful ones you push further. That takes a lot of exploration, and exploration needs fresh, independent thinking: agents biased by a failed attempt keep chasing the same failed route. So develop-project keeps the thrusts, or studies, as independent as possible, mechanically limiting each one to its own isolated folder. But a project can't be purely independent, or you never build on what you've found. So the skill balances independent thrusts with an established core of theory or experiments. Results enter the core through a promotion gate: robustly audited, coherent notation, a good interface, and, most importantly, your approval. Results that fail the gate stay outside, with their evidence kept. So the core holds results that are true and worthwhile, and new studies can branch off it and build on them. That's the answer to the gem in the haystack: if it's in the core, it's worth your time to read. And when you turn the work into a paper, review-paper audits the whole package from every angle: the logic, but also the citations, the literature and the novelty. It's what a researcher with real integrity would do to their own paper. That's the whole toolkit, so let me wrap up.

## 8. Simple ideas, a big difference, and just the beginning.
So to wrap up. All the ideas I've shared are very simple, but for me they made an enormous difference. And this is clearly just the beginning: there's so much room to improve and to fully leverage the models we already have. The table shows all the skills I mentioned, and they're in the repo. Feel free to use them, improve them, and add your own tricks for your domain. Thanks.

## 9. One question, or a whole project.
Now let's take a closer look at each of the five skills. As a reminder, they work at two scales. For a single, well-scoped question, formulate-problem makes it precise, solve-problem solves it, and teach-material makes sure you understand the result. For a whole project, develop-project runs many studies, each going through those three, and review-paper audits the finished paper. Let's go through them in that order.

## 10. Seal the loopholes first.
First, formulate-problem. A research question usually starts vague. Before anyone tries to solve it, the skill writes a research contract: the objects and definitions, the admissible domain, the order of the quantifiers, the conclusion and its scope, what counts as evidence, and which substitutions are forbidden. Only then does it state a sharp conjecture. And then it attacks its own statement, looking for the ways an agent could cheat: an easier target, a definition that drifts, a 'for all' quietly weakened to 'there exists', a proxy metric, or one example passed off as a whole class. Every loophole it finds gets closed, and the result is frozen as a contract, so no later step can quietly change the question.

## 11. Every step, checked.
solve-problem picks up that frozen question. It first takes the strongest reasonable reading, never a straw man. Before a long proof, it lays out the plan: the lemmas and how they combine. Then it derives every nontrivial step. When it relies on an external theorem, it states the exact form it needs and checks every hypothesis against the actual objects. It tests edge cases: zero, the boundary, degenerate cases. Then it audits its own draft, and any step that leans on 'clearly' gets a real derivation. Only then does the answer go out, and it answers exactly the question in the contract.

## 12. Light up every symbol.
The third stage is teach-material. Here's the kind of formula an agent might hand you: dozens of symbols packed into one line. The skill teaches it one block at a time. Each block introduces one new object, with its type, before using it, and as each block lands, another symbol in the formula becomes something you understand. Every claim carries its status: exact, an assumption, or a heuristic. And when you're confused, it doesn't append a one-line patch: it rebuilds the whole block. By the end, every symbol is lit.

## 13. Grow a core, one gate at a time.
So far, that's one question. A real project is many of them, and that's where develop-project comes in. From the research question, studies branch out. Each study has its own question, its permitted inputs, an owner and its own folder, and a study that should be independent doesn't get to see the others' work. Inside each study, the agents formulate, solve and learn. When a study has a result, it doesn't go straight into the project. It goes through a promotion gate: an independent review of the full argument and code, an integration check on notation, interfaces and tests, and your approval. Pass, and the result joins the established core, which grows with every promotion. Fail, and the study stays outside, but its evidence and its status are kept. What passes becomes something you can build on: new studies branch off it and rely on it as an accepted result. And when the core is ready to become a paper, it goes to the last skill.

## 14. A ledger of claims.
Finally, review-paper. It reads the whole paper and lifts out the three to five claims that matter most. For each one, it gathers evidence: it re-derives the key proofs, it retrieves the cited papers and checks that each citation is used correctly, and it audits the code for leakage and unfair baselines. It rates the plausibility of a conclusion separately from the soundness of its argument. Each flaw gets a severity, fatal, major or minor, and a fatal flaw cascades to every claim that depends on it. The verdict says whether the central message stands, and the confidence reflects only what was actually checked.
