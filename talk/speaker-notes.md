# Research with AI Agents: speaker notes

## 1. Open questions, many agents
Hi everyone, I'm Amir. I want to share how I use AI agents on open research questions these days: what went wrong, and the hacky tactics that fixed most of it for me. It isn't a principled framework, just practical stuff that works, and all of it is in a repo linked at the end.

## 2. Smart models fail beautifully.
We're in the middle of a transition: from writing every line of code and every paper ourselves, to handing more and more research to extremely capable models. That's harder than it sounds, because research fails silently. Smart models fail beautifully: the prose reads well, the figures look right, even the math looks clean, and somewhere an assumption was quietly swapped or the question was downgraded, and the whole thing is wrong. At one point it got bad enough that I went back to pen and paper. Then there's the volume. They produce code and theory far faster than you can check it, so you end up with a huge pile of results, a few gems hidden in a haystack, and no easy way to find them. The bottleneck becomes you. So the question for the rest of this talk is: how do you raise the signal-to-noise ratio, so that what reaches you is worth your time, and you stay in control? It starts with the basics.

## 3. Most of the value is in the basics.
None of this slide is my invention, and that's the point. These models are very smart, and it's still easy to have a horrible experience simply because you don't know what's possible. For me, this is the 80/20. Two things matter most. Leave the chat window: a coding agent like Codex or Claude Code runs inside your repo, so your papers, code and results are already in context. And run everything on one always-on machine you can reach from anywhere, so everything lives in one place and agents keep working overnight. Then four things worth reading up on. Skills: folders with a SKILL.md that the agent loads only when a task needs it. AGENTS.md, or CLAUDE.md in Claude Code: project rules, read at the start of every session. Routing: a skill links reference files that open only when needed, so the agent reads the one long paper it needs, not all ten. And subagents: fresh agents, each with a clean context. That last one deserves its own slide.

## 4. Many agents beat one long thread.
Here's the idea I think most people underestimate: many agents are fundamentally more powerful than one long thread. Not because they're faster. You pay for the same tokens and hit the same rate limits. The difference is conceptual. A single thread accumulates everything: every dead end, every earlier claim, your own enthusiasm. Once it makes an early mistake, everything after is anchored to it, and telling it to "be unbiased" doesn't undo that. Separate agents start clean and see only what you put in their folder, so you decide what each one knows. That makes their judgments independent: three can miss a flaw, and it only takes one to catch it. You can also give them roles a single thread can't hold at once, one proposes, one attacks, one checks the code, and let them explore different routes instead of converging on the first idea. It's asking ten people once instead of one person ten times. Two skills in the repo are built on this: investigate-conjectures runs independent proof routes and audits, and organize-research-project decides who sees what. Which brings me to how the skills fit together.

## 5. One question, or a whole project.
They work at two scales. Some work is a single question. For that there are two skills in sequence: investigate-conjectures turns a vague question into a sharp, faithful conjecture, and once you're happy with it, solve-math-rigorously solves it. But most research is a whole project, made of many such questions. There, organize-research-project develops the project, and review-ai-paper audits the finished paper. And the scales nest: every thrust in a project runs the one-question pair, and a review's findings can start the next round.

## 6. Formulate first, then solve.
Start with one question. Even with many agents, an open question gets downgraded: an agent finds a special case, a counterexample, or an assumption you didn't forbid, and solves that instead. Little tweaks that kill the question. And as work passes between agents, the goal drifts. So investigate-conjectures formulates before anything is solved. A precise question gets frozen as a contract every agent checks itself against. A vague one, which is the usual case, gets formulated by the model itself, faithful to the spirit of your question, and checked against a list of ways to cheat: an easier target, a weakened "for all", a proxy, one example standing in for a whole class. Before any claim counts, separate checks rebuild every step and hunt for counterexamples. Only then does solve-math-rigorously take over: the strongest reading, never a straw man, every step shown, every cited theorem's assumptions checked. That ends the back-and-forth of "this isn't what I asked" and "that's cheating".

## 7. Develop in isolation, then audit cold.
Now the whole project. Most thrusts fail, so each one lives in its own study, an isolated folder, running the one-question pair inside. My AGENTS.md keeps every agent inside its own study, because once an agent picks up a wrong approach, it tends to follow it forever. But pure isolation means reinventing the wheel, so there's also an established core of theory and code, and the heart of it is the promotion gate. A result gets in only after an independent audit, integration with the shared notation, tests and interfaces, and my approval. Many results fail, and that's fine: they stay outside, evidence kept, and nothing builds on them. What passes is high-signal, so when I read something in the core, it's worth reading properly. That's organize-research-project. Then, when the project becomes a paper, review-ai-paper audits it cold: a fresh reviewer gets the finished paper, not its history. It pulls the cited sources, audits the code, and builds a claim ledger, and its findings can start the next round.

## 8. Make them explain it to you.
Last, your own head. Even good results are hard to read, because model output is full of compressed notation: dozens of new symbols to say in a line what would take pages. So I have a skill whose only job is to teach me: teach-technical-math. It walks me through a paper, or a report the agents wrote, one block at a time, defining every symbol before it's used. And when I'm confused, it rebuilds the whole explanation instead of patching one sentence. The general lesson: write skills that teach the model how to talk to you.

## 9. Simple ideas, a big difference, and just the beginning.
So, to wrap up. These ideas are simple, and they made an enormous difference for me. But this is just the beginning: there's a lot more to exploit, and a lot of room to improve. The table is your map to the repo. The skills are plain markdown, written for Codex, and the same SKILL.md format works in Claude Code. So take whatever's useful, add your own, and please tell me your tricks. Thanks.
