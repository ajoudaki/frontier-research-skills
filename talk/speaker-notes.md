# Research with AI Agents: speaker notes

## 1. Many agents, one question
Hi everyone, I'm Amir. I want to share how I do research with AI agents these days: what went wrong, and the hacky tactics that fixed most of it for me. None of this is a principled framework. It's practical, and everything I show is in a repo you can use, linked at the end.

## 2. We have to learn new ways of writing theory, papers and code.
We're in the middle of a transition. Not long ago, we wrote our code and our papers ourselves, line by line. Now the models are extremely smart, and the opportunity is enormous: if we could really tap into that intelligence, research would move much faster. But we haven't learned the new way of working yet. We have to learn new ways of writing theory, papers and code, and that goes for experiments as much as for proofs. From what I hear, most of us are struggling with it. I certainly was.

## 3. I went back to pen and paper.
I've used these models since 2022, first for coding, and for coding they just kept getting better. Research is different, because research fails differently. When code is wrong, you usually see it: it doesn't run, a test fails, the plot looks off. When research is wrong, it can fail miserably and still look fine. So as the models got smarter, using them for research actually got harder, and at some point I gave up and went back to pen and paper. We used to do everything ourselves, so we knew exactly what was going on. Now so much is delegated that it's easy to lose track, and to lose control.

## 4. The bottleneck becomes you.
And losing track turns into the real bottleneck: you. These models produce code and theory much faster than you can read or check it. Some of it is wrong. Some of it isn't relevant. Some of it quietly answers a different question. So you end up with a huge pile of results, a few gems hidden in a haystack, and no easy way to find them. That can be overwhelming enough that you just give up. So here's the question for the rest of this talk: how do you raise the signal-to-noise ratio, so that what reaches you is worth your time, and you stay in control? Every trick I'll show is a filter on that pile. But first, a few basics.

## 5. Most of the value is in the basics.
None of this next part is my invention, and that's exactly the point. These models are very smart, and it's still easy to have a horrible experience, simply because you don't know what's possible. This slide is the 80/20, honestly more like 95/5: most of the value comes from these basics. The two big ones. First, leave the chat window: a coding agent like Codex or Claude Code runs inside your repo, so your papers, code and results are already in context, and you never re-upload anything. Second, run everything on one always-on machine you connect to from anywhere: one place where everything lives, and agents that keep working overnight. Then four things worth reading up on. Skills: folders with a SKILL.md file that the agent loads only when a task needs them. AGENTS.md, or CLAUDE.md for Claude Code: the project's rules, read at the start of every session. Routing: a skill can link reference files that are opened only when needed, so out of ten long papers, the agent loads only the one it needs. And subagents: fresh agents that the main agent spawns, each with a clean context. That last one deserves its own slide.

## 6. Many agents beat one long thread.
Here's the idea I think most people underestimate: many agents are fundamentally more powerful than one long thread. And it's not about speed. You pay for the same tokens either way, and the provider rate-limits you, so running agents in parallel doesn't really make you faster. The difference is conceptual. A single thread accumulates everything: every dead end, every earlier claim, your own enthusiasm. Once it makes a mistake early, everything after is anchored to it, and asking it to "be unbiased" or "start fresh" doesn't undo that. Separate agents are different by construction. Each one starts clean and sees only what you put in its folder, so you decide what it knows. That makes their judgments genuinely independent: one can catch what another missed. You can give them roles that one thread can't hold at the same time: one proposes, one attacks, one checks the code. And they can explore genuinely different routes instead of all converging on the first idea. It's the difference between asking one person ten times and asking ten people once. Two of the skills are built on this: investigate-conjectures runs independent proof routes and independent audits, and organize-research-project decides who sees what, so an independent attempt or reviewer starts fresh, without earlier verdicts.

## 7. Many agents can still lose your question.
But many agents don't make you immune. They can make you lose control even faster. Each agent can still downgrade your question: give it an open problem and it finds a special case, a counterexample, or an assumption you didn't forbid, and solves that instead. Little tweaks that kill the question. And as work passes from agent to agent, the original goal gets lost along the way. So there are two defenses, and investigate-conjectures has both. First, formulate before you solve. If the question is precise, freeze it as a contract that every agent checks itself against. In research it usually isn't precise, so the first stage uses the model's own intelligence to formulate it, faithful to the spirit of your question, and checks it against a list of ways to cheat: an easier target, a weakened "for all", a proxy, one example standing in for a whole class. If the question really has to change, the original stays frozen. Second, audit from different angles. Before a result counts, separate checks attack it: one reconstructs every step, one verifies the cited theorems and their assumptions, one hunts for counterexamples and edge cases, and one asks whether it still answers the original question. For papers, review-ai-paper does the same: it pulls the cited sources, audits the code, and builds a claim ledger. That's what keeps the agents on a short leash.

## 8. Many low-signal studies. One high-signal core.
The same idea, controlling what each agent sees, scales up to the whole project, and that's what organize-research-project is about. Research means pushing many thrusts in different directions, and most of them fail. So each one lives in its own study, an isolated folder, and my AGENTS.md tells every agent to stay inside its own study. Once an agent picks up a wrong approach, it tends to follow it forever; isolation stops that from spreading, and keeps exploration creative. But pure isolation means reinventing the wheel. So there's also an established core of theory and code. A result gets in only through promotion gates: an independent audit, plus integration, meaning shared notation, tests, and clean interfaces. And I approve every promotion myself. That core is where the signal is high. Future studies can build on its theorems and its code, and when I read something there, it's worth reading properly. The skill keeps three questions separate: is a result true, has it been checked, and has it been adopted. And it comes with templates for AGENTS.md and for each study.

## 9. Make them explain it to you.
The last filter is your own head. Even good results are hard to read, because model output is full of compressed notation: dozens of new symbols to say in a line what would take pages. So I have a skill whose only job is to make the model teach me: teach-technical-math. It teaches a paper, or a report the agents wrote, one block at a time, with every symbol defined before it's used. And when I'm confused, it doesn't add a one-line patch; it rebuilds the whole explanation. The general lesson: write skills that teach the model how to talk to you.

## 10. From a pile of output to results you trust.
So, to wrap up. The problem was a pile of output you can't check, and every idea was a way to filter it. Get the basics right. Use many agents, not one long thread. Keep them honest: formulate the question faithfully and audit from different angles, with investigate-conjectures and review-ai-paper. Promote only what survives into a trusted core, with organize-research-project. And make the result readable, with teach-technical-math. They're all in the repo, as plain markdown. They're written for Codex, and the same SKILL.md format works in Claude Code. These ideas made an enormous difference for me, but this is just the beginning of the road. There's a lot of room to improve, so take whatever's useful, add your own, and please tell me your tricks. Thanks.
