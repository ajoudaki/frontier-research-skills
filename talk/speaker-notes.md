# Research with AI Agents: speaker notes

## 1. Open-ended research with AI agents
Hi everyone, I'm Amir. Today's models are smart enough to do real research, but using them on open questions is surprisingly hard: they produce far more than we can check, and their mistakes are silent. In this short talk I'll share the practical habits I developed to do it without losing control. It isn't a principled framework, just what worked for me, mostly in theoretical research, and everything is in a repo linked at the end. Let me start with the problem.

## 2. Smart models fail beautifully.
Let's start with where we are today. We're in the middle of a fundamental transition: from writing every line of code, paper and theory ourselves, to delegating much more research to very capable models. The transition is hard, because in research, agents fail silently, and the smarter they get, the harder those failures are to catch. The text looks flawless, the figures look great, even the math looks great at a glance. But the real failure is hidden on page 12, in some assumption that quietly absorbs all the complexity. On top of that, they produce code, theory and papers far faster than we can read. So we end up with a pile of results that may contain some gems, but finding them takes an enormous amount of time: a gem in a haystack. And noise isn't only mistakes: a result can be correct but irrelevant. You lose so much control that it can defeat the purpose of using them at all. So how do we tackle this? We have to raise the signal-to-noise ratio. How do we do that?

## 3. Four simple foundations.
The first part of the answer is basic. Before we can look for any gem, we have to solve a simpler problem: context. So first, a coding agent, running on one central, always-on machine. Then the theory, the LaTeX, the code, the results and the figures are all in context, and you stop uploading things back and forth. Agents can work overnight, and even if you dispatch jobs elsewhere, the results come back to that one place. Second, persistence: put everything under version control. Not just the code, which most of us already do, but the theory, the notes, the audits, the paper. When you give agents freedom, things go wrong, or you change your mind, or you want a big refactor, and you can always roll back. Third, reuse. If you type the same prompt again and again, turn it into a skill, which the agent loads only when it needs it. What a project always needs goes in a default file, AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md, and a large skill can route to parts loaded on demand. And unlike a chat's memory, these are plain files, so they move with you to another model. Each of these is obvious on its own, but together they already create a lot of value. The fourth one is less obvious: many agents. It deserves its own slide.

## 4. Many agents beat one long thread.
Many agents deserve their own slide, because here's one conceptual point I think many people still underestimate: multiple agents are fundamentally more powerful than one long thread. Not because they're faster. You pay for the tokens either way, and you're rate-limited. The difference is conceptual. A single thread accumulates everything: every dead end, every earlier claim, every sentiment, enthusiasm or pessimism. Once it makes an early mistake, everything after it is anchored to it, and telling it to be unbiased can't undo that. It doesn't know how. Separate agents get a clean, independent start, especially if you isolate them and decide mechanically what they see. Each one explores its own branch, and a branch can split when it finds something worth following. Run four agents, ideally prompted for diversity. The first one misses the flaw. So does the second. The third catches it. The fourth misses again. And that's still a success: one catch is all you need. It's asking ten people instead of asking one person ten times. Most of the skills in this repo build on this. Let me show you how they fit together.

## 5. Five skills, from a vague question to a paper.
There are five skills, and I'll go through them in the order of a project. develop-project is the architecture: it runs a whole project, one big open question, as many separate thrusts. Every thrust then goes through three stages. formulate-problem turns its vague question into a precise one, then solve-problem solves it, with every step checked, and teach-material makes sure you actually understand the result. Finally, when the project's core becomes a paper, review-paper audits it, and its findings can start the next round. Let's start with the architecture.

## 6. Develop in isolation, build on what passes.
Here's the architecture. A project is one big open question, and develop-project rests on three ideas. From the research question, studies branch out, and the first idea is isolation: each study has its own question, inputs and folder, and by default it doesn't see the others' work. With your approval, one study can build on another. That way, an agent stuck on one failed idea can't drag the others into it, and you get the widest exploration. Inside each study, the agents formulate, solve and learn. The second idea is the promotion gate. A result enters the project's core only if it's correct, by an independent review; coherent, in notation, interfaces and tests; and relevant, which means your approval. These pass, and the core grows with each one. These fail and stay outside, with their evidence kept. So whatever is in the core is worth your time: that's the signal-to-noise ratio. The third idea: you build on the core. New studies can rely on promoted results, and they can be promoted in turn, so the core keeps growing. But each thrust is itself a research problem. Even a narrowed question is still open, and it leaves a lot of wiggle room. So inside a thrust, the first challenge is to formulate it.

## 7. Formulate the steelman, then freeze it.
Take one thrust. Its question usually starts vague, like this cloud. The danger is that a vague question leaves room for answers to an easier question, and an agent will happily take them. The goal is the steelman, not the straw man: the strongest version that stays faithful to what you meant. So formulate-problem writes a research contract, and each field rules something out. The question itself is pinned down, so an easier target doesn't count. The definitions are fixed, so they can't drift halfway through. The quantifiers keep their order, so 'for all' can't quietly become 'there exists'. The evidence standard says what counts, so a proxy metric doesn't. And the scope is explicit, so one example can't stand in for a whole class. Notice that the vague boundary couldn't tell us whether any of these were in or out. Once they're ruled out, the sharp boundary emerges: one precise conjecture. Then it's frozen. A material change needs your say, and results for a changed question never count as answers to the original. Now we know exactly what to solve. But solving it is still very hard, and that needs a second, strong push.

## 8. Solve by many routes, then audit the proof.
Solving starts from the frozen contract. With your go-ahead, the proof search runs as parallel agents, one per route, and each route uses a genuinely different mechanism: duality, induction, an extremal argument. They all get the same contract, and none is told which strategy to favor, so they stay independent. After every round, a registry keeps score. Two routes that turn out to use the same idea are merged. A route that needs a lemma as hard as the theorem itself is marked blocked, but its valid lemmas are kept. Mature routes can borrow from each other: here duality supplies the missing lemma, and induction becomes a candidate proof. But a candidate isn't a proof yet. Separate checks rebuild every implication from the contract, check each cited theorem and its hypotheses, and hunt for counterexamples at the boundary and in degenerate cases. Only what survives gets written up by solve-problem: the plan first, every nontrivial step derived, no 'clearly' in place of a reason, and an answer to exactly the question in the contract. Now we have a written proof that's most likely correct. But you're still the judge of whether it belongs in the core: whether it's relevant, and whether some assumption or technique was quietly tweaked along the way. And with a large budget, there can be many candidate results. So you have to understand them, and even correct results can be very hard to understand. That's the third stage.

## 9. Learn the idea first, then every step.
A correct proof you don't understand isn't worth much. Here's the kind of line an agent, or a paper, might hand you: private symbols everywhere. teach-material, together with explain-canonically, teaches it in layers. First, notation. It translates into the symbols the field already uses: the parameters theta, the loss L, its gradient. Only a few global symbols, each defined with its type before it's used. Then the idea, before any detail: every step goes downhill, and if the slope can't change too fast, each step lowers the loss by a definite amount. That's also the plan of the proof. Then a toy example that keeps the real structure: a one-dimensional quadratic, where you can see exactly when the iterates shrink to zero. And only then the details. When the argument invokes a theorem, it's stated right there, in the exact form needed, with its hypothesis checked and its conclusion spelled out for our objects. And if you're confused at any point, it rewrites the whole block rather than patching a sentence. Once you understand it, you can decide whether it belongs in the core. Now let's zoom back out to the whole project.

## 10. Audit every claim against its evidence.
Back to the big picture. Once the core is understood, all of it, or part of it, becomes a coherent result, and that result has to be written as a paper. A paper is more than its proofs: it has a literature, a background, a claim of novelty and of significance. That needs a different kind of audit. review-paper reads the full submission, code included. It lifts out the three to five claims that matter most, into a ledger. Then it checks each one against its evidence. It re-derives the key proofs: here, the key lemma's proof fails. It retrieves the cited papers and checks that each citation is used correctly: one holds, and one source can't be verified. It audits the code: these baselines are unfair. It rates how plausible each conclusion is separately from how sound its argument is. Each flaw gets a severity: fatal, major or minor. And a fatal flaw cascades to every claim that depends on it: the main theorem falls with the lemma. The verdict says whether the central message stands, and the confidence reflects only what was actually checked. And its findings can start the next round of the project. So let's go back to where we started.

## 11. From the haystack to the gem.
Remember the gem in the haystack? Here's what a real project looks like over time. From the research question, studies branch out. Most lines of work go one step at a time; only now and then does one split. Some pass the gate, and the core forms around them, like a lake. Studies keep going. By default they don't see each other, but with your approval one can build on another, even on work that hasn't been promoted yet. So the explored region keeps growing, while the core grows slowly, and always in one piece. In the end, the core is about a fifth of everything: an ocean of knowledge that's true and relevant. And around everything we explored there's another shore, where the uncharted waters begin. But a paper isn't the whole core. You carve out a coherent part, grown from the original question, and it has to pass new gates: novelty and significance. That's the audit. So out of 150 studies, 10 become the paper. That's the gem in the haystack. So let me wrap up.

## 12. Simple ideas, a big difference, and just the beginning.
So to wrap up. All the ideas I've shared are very simple, but for me they made an enormous difference. And this is clearly just the beginning: there's so much room to improve and to fully leverage the models we already have. The table shows all the skills I mentioned, and they're in the repo. Feel free to use them, improve them, and add your own tricks for your domain. Thanks.
