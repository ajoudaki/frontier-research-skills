# Adversarial Audit

Use this reference before calling a result decisive, closing a loophole, declaring a route impossible, or claiming that only technical work remains.

## Audit protocol

For every central claim or proposed next step, write:

| Item | Question |
|---|---|
| Target claim | What exact statement is under attack? |
| Strongest alternative | What is the most plausible competing explanation or obstruction? |
| Failure signature | What observation or derivation would distinguish it? |
| Cheapest discriminator | What is the smallest valid theory or experiment check? |
| Validity gates | What would make the check numerically or logically uninterpretable? |
| Consequence | Which claim-ladder rung changes under each outcome? |

Attack the strongest viable alternative. Do not use a weak baseline merely because it is easy to defeat.

## Structural attacks

### Hidden information and memory

- Check whether coefficients, initialization, or forcing use the future target trajectory.
- Check whether a finite coordinate hides an arbitrary function, history, matrix, dataset, or unbounded-precision code.
- Check whether the current state determines future evolution or whether untracked response/history variables survive.
- Test restartability from an intermediate state when autonomy is claimed.

### Nonclosure and tail escape

- Identify the exact residual generated by truncation.
- Check whether bounded norm implies compactness in the required topology; usually it does not.
- Check whether high modes can feed low observables through multiplication, contraction, transpose, aliasing, or nonlinear interaction.
- Distinguish small individual coefficients from a small collective tail.
- Ask for a reachable-state estimate rather than a bound on an arbitrary ambient ball.

### Error production versus propagation

- Locate where omitted error is produced.
- Locate the stability or propagator estimate that transports it.
- Reject arguments that provide only stability but no small source term.
- Check for nonnormal or transient amplification even when eigenvalues appear stable.

### Limits and topology

- Write the limit order explicitly.
- Check every interchange of limit, expectation, derivative, integral, optimization, or long-time operation.
- Verify tightness, uniform integrability, regularity, or compactness assumptions needed for the interchange.
- Confirm that convergence in the chosen topology controls the named observables.
- Separate compact-time results from all-time claims.

### Witness versus existence

- Determine whether a failure concerns the chosen basis, closure, solver, or state.
- Check whether a modest admissible enrichment could repair the witness.
- Do not use failure of one construction as a no-go theorem for the full approximation class.

## Mundane-explanation attacks

Construct the strongest scientifically plausible nuisance explanation, such as:

- effective linearity after matching gain, scale, spectrum, or another nuisance parameter;
- frozen-feature, lazy, low-rank, or small-motion behavior;
- interpolation rather than autonomous prediction;
- observable insensitivity despite wrong internal dynamics;
- a special symmetry or data degeneracy;
- an easier regime introduced by the testbed.

Use a matched control that grants the nuisance explanation its best legitimate parameters without giving it the target trajectory. Add a mechanism-specific ablation when possible.

Closing a loophole requires evidence against this strongest version, not against identity dynamics or another straw man.

## Numerical and empirical attacks

- Check conditioning before interpreting high-order behavior.
- Use a common reference when comparing successive approximations.
- Separate discretization, quadrature, sampling, optimization, and model errors.
- Replicate any conclusion that is comparable to numerical variability.
- Freeze primary metrics and thresholds before inspecting the decisive result.
- Track negative and failed runs, not only favorable configurations.
- Distinguish a theory-selected stress test from a searched favorable example.
- Check source hashes, configurations, seeds, and raw-result provenance.
- Treat failure of a validity gate as inconclusive, not as evidence for either hypothesis.

## Logical severity

Classify each surviving issue:

- **Fatal:** contradicts the central conjecture under its stated contract.
- **Witness-fatal:** defeats the current construction but not the existence claim.
- **Major:** blocks a necessary bridge between claim-ladder rungs.
- **Conditional:** matters only if a named assumption fails.
- **Minor:** affects constants, presentation, or a noncentral extension.

Do not call a gap “technical” unless a known theorem or fully specified argument appears to close it under already verified hypotheses.

## Evidence language

Use these distinctions:

- **Ruled out:** logically contradicted or rejected by a valid, sufficiently powered discriminator over the stated scope.
- **Strongly disfavored:** survives logically but conflicts with replicated evidence.
- **Disfavored:** has contrary evidence with meaningful caveats.
- **Unsupported:** lacks positive evidence.
- **Open:** viable evidence exists on both sides or the decisive check is missing.
- **Inconclusive:** the attempted check failed a logical or numerical validity gate.

“No failure observed” is not “ruled out.” “Accurate on tested observables” is not “mechanism identified.”

## Final hostile questions

Before upgrading the research claim, answer:

1. What is the strongest surviving ordinary explanation?
2. What is the strongest structural obstruction?
3. What numerical artifact could mimic the result?
4. Which necessary claim-ladder bridge remains assumed?
5. Would the opposite result have changed the conclusion?
6. Was that decision rule fixed before observing the result?
7. Does the evidence generalize beyond the selected instances?
8. What would still make the central claim false?
